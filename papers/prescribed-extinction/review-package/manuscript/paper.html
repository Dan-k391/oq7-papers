Abstract

For two opposite real linearly polarized plane waves, reciprocity fixes the extinction of a compact scatterer but permits different absorption and scattering. At a fixed vacuum wavenumber k>0k>0, we establish that the supremum of the absorption difference divided by common extinction is one at every exactly prescribed extinction E*>0E_*>0. The realization uses finite compact two-layer local anisotropic nonmagnetic dielectrics satisfying arbitrary fixed upper caps P>1P>1 and Q>0Q>0, with I≼Re⁡Hε≼PII\preceq\operatorname{Re}_{\!H}\varepsilon\preceq PI and 0≺Im⁡Hε≼QI0\prec\operatorname{Im}_{\!H}\varepsilon\preceq QI in each layer. Every individual body has asymmetry strictly below one. The constructive step is a physical aperture limit uniform after factorization by the squared sine of a material rotation angle, including its zero-angle auxiliary limit. This permits the coupling angle to decrease as the aperture increases while total extinction remains fixed. A subsequent positive isotropic-loss perturbation and a second intermediate-value argument preserve the exact target. Geometric resources are unrestricted across the approximating sequence, and no common positive loss floor or bandwidth is imposed. The polarization mechanism and reciprocal extinction identity have prior precedents; the contribution is the controlled compact Maxwell realization at prescribed extinction. A frozen Lean 4 formalization certifies the stated existence, suprema, and physical power identities, with the scope of its verification distinguished from independent semantic and priority review.

1 Introduction and relation to OQ7

Open Question 7 in the tutorial of Miller and Monticone asks for fundamental limits on the redistribution between absorption and scattering under time-reversed illumination [1]. A normalization, illumination class, and resource class are needed to turn that question into a theorem. Here the normalization is the common dimensional plane-wave extinction, the illumination pair is opposite real linear polarization in vacuum, and the objects are local reciprocal dielectric bodies at one frequency.

The result is an existence and supremum theorem. It states that a finite object can have exactly any prescribed positive extinction and an absorption difference arbitrarily close to that extinction, while its real permittivity and loss obey fixed pointwise upper bounds. It establishes neither a finite-resource optimum nor finite-bandwidth performance. The same material tensor is used in both experiments, and scattering includes the whole outgoing sphere and both polarizations.

At a fixed coupling angle, enlarging a planar-like aperture increases extinction proportionally to area. A fixed-stack aperture limit therefore does not suffice to prescribe total extinction. The exact rotation factorization and uniform normalized aperture theorem below permit the coupling angle to decrease while the aperture grows. Green-operator and localization arguments justify this passage for actual finite Maxwell bodies. Dipole optimization, scalar thickness laws, fixed-resource bounds, and quartic small-size results have different hypotheses and are outside the present proof.

2 Prior work and the precise contribution

Reciprocal extinction equality and directional absorption are established facts. Sounas and Alù derived opposite-incidence extinction symmetry and its implications for scattering manipulation [2]. Hopkins and coauthors studied directional absorption and scattering at equal extinction through modal interference in reciprocal particles [3]. The passive inequality used below is a consequence of power conservation, and its strictness follows from the optical theorem. We claim no independent discovery of these facts.

Polarization conversion combined with selective attenuation also has a substantial history. Armitage gives reciprocity constraints for Jones matrices and a polarizer–waveplate example [4]. Khurgin and coauthors demonstrated reciprocal asymmetric polarization switching using selective attenuation and imperfect waveplates, including passive experiments [5]. This is a closely related channel mechanism. Ra’di, Asadchy, and Tretyakov analyze thin bianisotropic absorbers and distinct opposite-side responses, distinguishing reciprocal and nonreciprocal constitutive classes [6]. Such sheet and channel constructions establish relevant mechanisms; their realizability assumptions must be retained when comparing them with local nonmagnetic dielectric volumes. Sounas and Alù also derive multiport and free-space response bounds, including a stated extension to loss through additional channels, as well as their lossless two-port result [7]. These necessary bounds do not themselves construct the prescribed-extinction dielectric family.

Andersen and Frandsen showed that receiving-antenna absorption divided by absorption plus total scattering can approach one, with a directivity-dependent cost [8]. That result precedes any general near-unit absorption-efficiency claim. It does not by itself supply the present simultaneous opposite-incidence response at every prescribed extinction within a capped local dielectric class. Schab, Gustafsson, and collaborators formulate absorption–scattering tradeoffs with prescribed losses and material parameters [9]; those finite-resource optimization problems motivate the resource distinctions maintained here. Khurgin and Sun’s loss-scaling analysis supplies separate material and confinement context for resonant metal–dielectric structures [10].

Related layered and metasurface devices also require explicit comparison. Kononchuk and coauthors combine dichroic attenuation with magnetic Faraday rotation in a layered-sheet isolator [11]; its complete device has a different, magnetically nonreciprocal constitutive class, while its selective-attenuation component is relevant. Earlier bianisotropic metasurface asymmetric absorption was studied by Yazdi and coauthors [12]. Máñez-Espina and coauthors derive maximum directional absorptance difference in a reciprocal bianisotropic metasurface model and verify its predictions with full-wave simulations [13]. A planar absorptance difference in a specified coupled-mode model is distinct from a compact dimensional extinction-normalized supremum with every positive target and arbitrary material caps. These comparisons do not establish first priority for the latter theorem.

For the finite-to-planar passage, Gao, Yang, and Kattawar studied polarized extinction of plates with large aspect ratios [14]. Gustafsson and coauthors derived planar extinction and scattered-transmission identities for periodic slabs [15]; literal compact-material truncation and the uniform normalized limit required here have different quantifiers. Windowed Green-function methods rigorously treat layered backgrounds [16, 17], while volume-integral formulations supply the singular-kernel and radiation framework [18, 19]. A computational window applied to an infinite-interface equation and literal truncation of a material body are different operations. Our smooth windows enter only trial currents; the final material aperture remains sharp.

Mann and Alù’s 2023 conference abstract explicitly announces maximal directional absorption for bianisotropic dipolar particles [20]. It is direct prior art for the qualitative extremal phenomenon. The full equations, normalization, and any equivalent compact dielectric realization have not been verified from that contribution. Priority against it remains unresolved. More generally, absence of an identical retrieved statement does not establish first priority.

The contribution established here is the combination of an aperture theorem uniform at vanishing physical coupling, exact finite-object extinction tuning, and positive-loss regularization preserving arbitrary fixed upper material caps. The mechanism, passive bound, extinction symmetry, and planar optical theorem have the prior precedents described above.

The accompanying targeted theorem-level priority comparison separates known ingredients and routine adaptations from the potentially distinct normalized compact-localization and exact-target combination. It does not establish first priority. Full-primary access and theorem equivalence remain unresolved for the identified Mann–Alù and Gao–Yang–Kattawar contributions and the stated version-specific access gaps.
